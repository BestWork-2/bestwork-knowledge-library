THE IMPORTANCE OF DATA TO EFFECTIVE SELECTION

Businesses have traditionally had two types of problems: systems

problems and people problems. Systems problems are generally much

easier to solve. This is because systems issues involve objective data that

is usually quantifiable. Also, in dealing with systems, everyone is using a

common frame of reference, with defined terminology and definitions. With

people problems, the situation is completely different. The data is almost

always subjective. Descriptions of the same people problem can vary

dramatically depending upon the point of view of the observer. Solutions

are often based on strongly held opinions born of different circumstances

with different people. These opinions, while valid for one person, frequently

prove untenable when applied to another. People issues are further

complicated by the emotions of the participants, which can mask or distort

the facts. All in all, an extraordinary amount of management time is spent

each day trying to get smart, hard working employees to effectively do the

job that they were hired to do. Now we know why this happens. And we

know how to prevent it.

Job performance has always been understood through a two part

paradigm. An employee needed the right Attitude for the job. This was a

combination of the individual’s values, ethics, and general outlook on life

and work. An employee also needed the right Skills for the job. This was a

combination of the individual’s experience, knowledge, and training. This

paradigm is what determined management’s response to unsatisfactory

performance. If an employee appeared to have the right Attitude a n d

worked hard, the problem appeared to be a lack of Skills. Consequently,

management used additional coaching and training to boost performance,

and the problem was described as being part of the learning curve.

Sometimes this was successful and sometimes it was not. If an employee

had the right Skills, the problem must be one of Attitude or motivation.

Consequently, management used a variety of motivational strategies,

ranging from offering bonuses to threatening dismissal. Sometimes this

was successful and sometimes it was not.

Over time, countless theories and methodologies were offered to explain or

improve these results. Some said better management techniques were

needed, and better ones were developed. They still worked just sometimes.

Some suggested that underperforming employees had reached their level

of incompetence. Others explained that some people just did not want to

work, and nothing could help them. This pessimistic view seemed

unfounded, since virtually everyone woke up in the morning wanting to

achieve things. Motivational philosophies abounded, but the effects, when

they occurred, were short-lived.

In the 90’s, several new psychometric instruments were developed

specifically for use within the business world. It became possible to easily

and accurately measure how people would behave in job situations and

how they processed information. More importantly, the traits and abilities

that were being measured by the new instruments were not trainable,

coachable, or effectively changeable in any way. These systems were also

designed to correlate the data with job performance. The explosion of

research revealed conclusively exactly how dependent job performance

was on the employee’s core personality traits and cognitive abilities. When

this third component of job performance was combined with Attitude and

Skills, the cornerstones were complete. When Job Fit was understood, it

was easy to see why some people struggled in certain positions after being

successful in others. It was easy to see why smart people, working hard,

still failed in certain jobs. And it was possible to see why even well

designed training, motivation, and coaching were often not successful.

Skills and Attitude were as important as always, but the third factor, Job

Fit, was the one that opened the door to so many opportunities for

managers and business owners. Attitude could be influenced with

management style and the working environment. Skills could definitely be

learned, and experience could be acquired. It was Job Fit that tended to

remain the same despite coaching, training, and various incentives. This

meant that managers could focus on developing positions that took

advantage of each individual’s particular strengths. Training programs

could focus on skills and knowledge issues rather than questionable

performance problem solving. Managers could understand from the first

day how best to direct a new employee with the kind of information the new

tools provided. The findings even challenged many classic career paths by

showing how moving from one job to another often demanded dramatically

different core behaviors, sometimes even opposite extremes of behavior. It

explained classic promotion problems such as the top salesperson who

Selection and recruiting were forever changed. The most effective

interviewing techniques of interviewing professionals could not match the

results of average managers using the new technologies. Several of the

newest products even provided behavioral event-based interview questions

matched to the behavioral profile of the individual candidates and the job.

This enabled managers to conduct focused and effective interviews

without extensive training. It also tended to standardize the interview

process across a diverse range of interviewers with varying skill levels. This

was a major benefit in terms of documenting objective and

nondiscriminatory hiring practices in compliance with current legal

regulations.

The biggest challenge for businesses seeking to use this new technology is

how to fit it into the existing hiring program. While different businesses have

different situations with different components, it is possible to offer practical suggestions and guidelines that will work for almost any situation.

Some Basic Guidelines for Effective Selection Systems

- Remember that you cannot train your way out of a bad hiring decision.

- The cost of hiring one poor employee is far greater than the cost of

having an effective selection process.

- Using new psychometrics technology by itself will avoid poor hires more

dependably than the most extensive interviews by the most talented

interviewers using the best techniques for interviewing.

- These psychometrics will dependably screen out people who cannot do

the job. They do not dependably point out star performers. The good news

is that you make money with average employees in a well run business. It

is the poor performers that destroy profits.

- Use the least expensive and most accurate screen first. For example, if

a 25 minute tool that costs a few dollars will screen out poor performers, do

that before spending valuable interview time with someone who is unlikely

to be able to succeed at the job anyway.
