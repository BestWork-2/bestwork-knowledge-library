## Page 1

Beyond Competency Models

or

Lead With Your Strengths… If You Know Them

Businesses often develop a set of competencies as a means of defining the

key elements of a particular job or a type of role (e.g. leadership,

management, customer service). These competencies serve as descriptors

of characteristics to seek when selecting individuals for those roles. Some

examples of competencies are: Strategic Focus Customer-Orientation People Management Process Management Successful Execution Focus on Results Adaptability Strategic Leadership Understanding Complexity Communication Entrepreneurial Thinking Market Orientation Collaboration Coping Skills Developing Others Empathetic Outlook Concrete Organization Accurate Listening There are as many flavors of these as there are flavors of ice cream, and just like ice cream, all of them are good to some extent. There is no doubt that there is value in identifying the competencies associated with success in the key positions within an enterprise.

However, two significant problems derive from this process. First, the

means of assessing the competencies within either existing employees or

job candidates are less than reliable. With existing employees, a rating

system of some type is used. The individual’s manager reflects upon the

year’s many events in conjunction with whatever objective metrics that

exist, and then assigns a subjective value to each respective competency.

Under the best of circumstances, this provides a hazy picture of the

individual’s capabilities. With job candidates, interviews are designed

around the competencies with questions aimed at exploring those areas.

This, of course, is fraught with the myriad failings of all subjective interview

processes.

The second problem is more pervasive. The creation of a set of

competencies predicates the need for each person to excel in each

competency. Therefore, a training program is usually established with the

goal of elevating each person’s proficiency in those competencies where

they are less than excellent. Unfortunately, some of those competencies

are dependent upon hard-wired traits or abilities. This sets up a cycle of

frustration as time, energy and money is spent in a misdirected effort which

ignores a person’s strengths and instead, focuses on pursuing the unicorn

of perfection in every area.

Baseball offers a comparative model that illustrates a more effective

understanding of strengths and how to maximize the potential of individual

performers. The principal competencies of baseball are hitting, pitching,

fielding, catching and running the bases. If a person’s strength is in

pitching, the manager puts them on the pitcher’s mound. Generally,

pitchers are not good hitters or superior base runners, however baseball

managers do not set up training programs to correct this. One league does

not even require pitchers to hit. Instead baseball managers concentrate the

training on developing the strength of the player.

Discussing competencies as a means of defining the critical elements of a

particular role can be a productive exercise. It is when those competencies

are presented as a recipe for success for individuals that the exercise

becomes counterproductive. It is important to honor the strengths of each

individual first, assessing which competencies are best supported by those

strengths.
