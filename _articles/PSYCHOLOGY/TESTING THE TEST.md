Testing the Test

How Do I Know It Works?

At some point, most successful businesses, seeking to improve their talent

acquisition, reduce turnover, enhance teamwork, increase sales or resolve

some other human resources issue, decide to purchase assessments.

Unfortunately, in most of these cases, they are sold an assessment, rather

than selecting the best one for their particular application or choosing one

based on quality differences. There are two primary reasons for this. First,

there are over 80,000 occupationally-related assessments in the

marketplace. There is no “assessment fair” or central shopping area where

the buyer can compare the various offerings. Usually, a salesperson shows

up or a resource is referred by a colleague or friend. That colleague or

friend is too often simply shifting their decision to you, albeit sincerely and

meaning well, but also lacking in any depth of critical evaluation.

Why would a true professional not critically evaluate such an important

decision? That is the other glitch in the “What test?” scenario. Many tests

look alike. They use similar terminology. The sales pitches promise similar

benefits. How do they look? Some are impressive, with multicolor graphs

and clever graphic designs. Some are relatively pedestrian in form and

layout. Often, the salesperson is the only person who seems to know

anything, and what they know, is that whatever they have is right for you.

Of course, it is usually the only thing they know, and most would be

challenged to name and discuss the features of any other products.

So, what to do? The obvious answer is to take the test yourself and see if

you think it is accurate. In fact, many low end products encourage it and

even offer it right off the bat. The reason for that is an interesting

psychological phenomenon called the P.T. Barnum Effect. This refers to the

fact that when presented with a fairly generalized description of personality,

most individuals will find it to be a reasonable match to themselves,

regardless of how accurate is the data that it is based upon. Fortune tellers

and horoscope writers have thrived on this type of writing.

The parallel option is to test some people you know and see if you think it

describes them accurately. Obviously, this plan travels down the same track

as the aforementioned Barnum Effect and consequently hits the same

problems, but there is a trickier element here. The latest tests measure

personality traits and cognitive abilities at a much deeper level than early

observed job behaviors at a specific and actionable level.

EXAMPLE: A common product that leans heavily on the “test yourself and

people you know” sales approach is one of the many versions of DISC. A

High D, according to their model, is highly assertive and decisive. It then

makes sense to correlate the observed assertive behavior with the DISC

product’s High D results. Today, with more advanced psychometric

measures, it is clear that, to paraphrase Satchel Paige, “It just ain’t

necessarily so.”

Observed “highly assertive and decisive” behavior can come from an

unassertive individual if: •They are wired for high compliance and are faced with a non- compliance issue and a situation with someone wanting to make an exception.

•They are highly outgoing people and are dealing with an audience of

introverts, who show little response to their enthusiasm and

talkativeness.

•They are highly sensitive and are under stress.

•They are highly competitive and are competing for an individual

reward.

•They are learn quickly and process information quickly and they are

talking about their expertise with others who are not their equals in that

area and who may learn more slowly.

•And many combinations of all of these.

Seeing a particular behavior is not as valuable as understanding exactly

what caused it. The first makes for an interesting discussion. The second

leads to a practical solution. The doctor must know more than you have a

headache in order to prescribe the right thing to make it go away.

The point of this digression is that if the only thing you are looking for is to

see if the report seems accurate, almost anything will look OK.

A more effective way to “test the test” is to use it to solve a real problem or

answer a real question in the business.

•Why is this salesperson not selling?

•What keeps this manager from developing their people?
