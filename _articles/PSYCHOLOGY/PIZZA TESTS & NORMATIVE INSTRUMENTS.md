## Page 1

Pizza Tests & Normative Instruments or Flashy vs. Real

I was recently asked about the quality of a new assessment-driven online hiring

application, and this was my reply.

There are 2 basic types of assessments. The older and simplest is to create a

model of different types of personality/approach to life/work style/etc. I call

these pizza tests, as there is not a wrong way to slice a pizza. Neither is there a wrong way to sort a population into groups. The questionnaire sorts the participants into the model, affording various comparisons and insights into the differences in people. The catch is that nothing is actually being measured. The same model methodology is used by DISC, Myers-Briggs, Strengths Finder, Eniagram and hundreds more.

The other type of assessment is a normative assessment. This means that a

construct of personality traits based on the universally accepted personality

model, the Big Five, is designed. Then, items are developed to tap into the

specific traits in that construct. When this is done in a rigorous manner and

statistically analyzed with factor analysis showing that each trait’s scale

demonstrates a normal bell curve or a normal distribution among the general

population, it, in effect, creates an instrument which measures the traits of any individual. Those measurements become a common language for describing behaviors, and this language can then be applied to job behaviors or classroom behaviors or anything that involves human behavior. That common language can describe both the individual’s strengths and in the same metrics, describe the strengths needed for any job.

Many of the online apps are a simple models with a clever questioning format

sorting participants into that model. All of that is wrapped in an equally clever marketing story (developed at Harvard, 30+ years of research, cost of bad hires).

It will make money because businesses are desperate to solve hiring problems

and this offers a simple and appealing solution. There are many “of these

offerings, funded by investors who cannot tell the difference between marketing

flash and real science.
