The Science Behind the DATA

The desire to understand how personality affects human behavior has been

around since the Greeks, when Hippocrates proposed the simple model

that is the basis of the hundreds of DISC-type instruments and the

countless numbers of assessments based on adjective checklists. In the

first half of the last century, there were other models, with the most

common being Myers-Briggs, an interesting version dreamed up by a

mother and daughter who “had observed a lot of behavior” and based their

model on Karl Jung’s theories, who himself considered it to be “an

interesting parlor game.” The survival of these models and in fact, their

In the 50’s, serious psychometric instruments were developed which were

based upon more scientifically sound personality models. These were

designed for clinical use and were unsuitable for use in the business world,

but they brought a world of new information into the field of psychology.

During the next three decades, research clearly established that there were

hard-wired personality traits that could be used to predict human behavior.

They remained essentially the same throughout a person’s adult life. In

occupational terms, these traits effectively did not change with training,

management or incentives. With the advent of computers, the capabilities

for deeper statistical analysis only strengthened these theories. Multiple

researchers all identified essentially the same set of foundational

personality traits, now referred to as The Big Five.

In 1993, The Prevue Assessment was published, combining a robust

assessment of cognitive abilities with a personality assessment based upon

The Big Five. It was designed to correlate these characteristics with job

behavior. The findings were astounding. Working with over two hundred

companies and job positions that ranged form executive level to production,

and across a wide assortment of industries, data from The Prevue was

used to match job candidates with specific jobs based on the cognitive and

personality strengths that were needed on the jobs. That was determined

by testing a sample of top performers to create a template or profile needed

for that job. Candidates for that job were then compared to the profile by

calculating to what percentage the candidate resembled the top

performers. Using only this gross matching process, the productivity across

all job categories increased by an average 25%. The results were

outstanding, but the company was never able to scale their business to a

significant level for several reasons.

- Independent distributor model limited marketing options

- Limited to hiring events by profiling methodology

- Awkwardness of building the initial profiles

- High price per unit limited penetration into the enterprise

- Need for experts to use beyond simple hiring

There are two fundamentally different sets of skills or abilities that impact

on-the-job success: technical skills or competencies and personal &

interpersonal competencies. Technical skills are the specific learned skills,

knowledge, and abilities required to do the work, that is, the skills learned

through formal education, training, or on-the-job experience. Obviously, it

would be impossible for any single instrument to measure the multitude of

technical skills required for success on the job. In any event, measuring

these skills is better done through an in-depth analysis of the person’s

resumé, thorough background and reference checks, and by work samples

and portfolio reviews.

There is, however, one basic factor that underlies all technical skills and is

essential to all jobs. That is cognitive ability, or intelligence. The im-

portance of intelligence to on-the-job performance has been highlighted by

Seligman (1997) who reported that the overwhelming body of research

supports the conclusion that “Intelligence matters in all jobs,” although how

much it matters depends on the nature of the job. By intelligence we mean

the speed of thinking; how readily new material is learned; and how quickly

underlying patterns are recognized and decisions made in response to

those patterns. In the development of the BestWork DATA instruments, an

early decision was made to include a measure of the participant’s cognitive

skills, especially in the areas of fluid intelligence, inductive reasoning, and

general sequential understanding, as well as quantitative understanding

(Horn & Noll, 1997). On the basis of both research and professional expe-

rience, it was clear that these particular cognitive skills were important to

on-the-job success, regardless of the job to be performed. A more ex-

tended discussion of the bases for these decisions as well as a description

of the process by which the Cognitive scale was developed can be found

below.

The Cognitive scale of the BestWork DATA instruments is largely based

upon Guildford’s Structure of Intellect model in which it is assumed that

cognitive ability or intelligence is best understood as a composite of several

separate abilities. While it is beyond the scope of this paper to present the

Guilford model, it seemed clear to us that not all of those abilities have a

direct impact upon workplace performance. The research (see Flanagan,

Genshaft, & Harrison (1997) for comprehensive review of this literature)

and our professional experience led us to select two of nine abilities, Fluid

Intelligence/Reasoning and Quantitative Reasoning/ Knowledge, for

inclusion in the BestWork DATA instruments. The omission of Crystallized

Intelligence/ Knowledge was a deliberate one. Although we were aware of

For the Fluid Reasoning ability, two sub-areas were included:

- General sequential reasoning (the ability to start with stated rules,

premises, or conditions, and to engage in one or more steps to

reach a solution to a problem)

- Induction (the ability to discover the underlying characteristic, e.g.,

rule concept, process, trend of class membership, that governs a

problem or as set of materials

- Speed of reasoning (the ability to solve problems quickly)

For the Quantitative Reasoning/Knowledge ability, two sub-areas were in-

cluded:

- Quantitative reasoning (the ability to inductively and deductively rea-

- Mathematical knowledge (the ability to solve a range of mathematical

problems)

These scales combine to present a remarkably robust measure of general

reasoning speed, which has long been accepted as a key factor in

workplace success in virtually all types of jobs.

in job success. Some of these are personal, how the individual approaches

work and other situations, such as conscientiousness, and some are

interpersonal, such as how friendly or agreeable the individual is in dealing

The Personality Construct Model

The never-ending list of descriptors of human behavior, the dimensions of

human personality, has long frustrated psychologists, who believe that a

small set of major traits must underlie all of these terms. The research of

over half a century has provided psychologists with strong evidence of the

existence of five basic factors or sets of traits independent of gender, race,

socioeconomic level, and even culture, the so-called “Big Five” (McRae &

Costa, 1997; Goodstein & Lanyon, 1999). The research generally shows

that sophisticated statistical analyses of both behavioral ratings and

personality test items yield the same five factors and that these factors are

quite stable over time. The research literature usually lists the five as

Neuroticism, Extroversion, Openness to Experience, Agreeableness, and

Conscientiousness (NEOAC). And there is strong research evidence that

The Big Five. In thinking about these five factors it is important to

remember that they, like all biological and psychological variables, are

normally distributed. That is, most people cluster around the average with

only a few individuals at the extremes. In describing the dimensions we

typically describe the extremes in order to make the dimension clear, but

few people will be found at these extremes and few jobs that require such

extremes of behavior. Let us now examine each of the five factors in turn.

Neuroticism refers to how the person responds to stress. Non-neurotic or

emotionally stable persons are able to maintain a mature, problem-solving

attitude while dealing with a wide range of stress conditions such as

interpersonal conflict, hazardous conditions, personal rejection, hostility,

and time pressures. Neurotic or emotionally unstable people breakdown

under even modestly stressful circumstances, becoming distraught, tearful,

anxious, self-doubting, and unable to handle the ordinary task demands.

Jobs differ in the degree of stress regularly experienced on a daily basis.

Some jobs, like police officers and fire fighters have jobs that have been

described as 59 minutes of boring routine, punctuated by one minute of

absolute terror. Maintaining one’s composure during that moment of terror

is essential to be able to both do the job successfully and to survive. While

it is difficult to think of jobs that are best filled by neurotic persons, most

jobs have only modest levels of stress and can be managed successfully

by persons with a wide range of stress tolerance. But it is important to

make sure that those jobs that have their occasional moments of terror

(and there are more of them than are readily apparent) are filled with per-

sons who have the necessary levels of stability.

Extroversion is the degree to which a person is energized by others, is

active, gregarious, sociable, and talkative. At the other end of the scale,

introversion, we find people who prefer solitude to the company of others,

who tend to be isolates, shy, reserved, reluctant to engage others, and

quiet. In your office, the person who is most likely to be the organizer of the

party, the after work sports events, and other interpersonal activities is

Some jobs, like sales of all sorts, require a high degree of extroversion

while there are many jobs that are best filled by introverts, for example,

writing computer code, copy editing, and many analytic and writing jobs.

Most sales managers understand that their best outside sales people have

boundless energy and seem to be impervious to rejection, characteristics of

the extrovert.

Openness to new experience describes people who are curious,

interested in new things, broad minded, creative, spontaneous, original,

and have a high tolerance for ambiguity. At the opposite end of the scale

we find people who are dogmatic in their thinking, unimaginative, concrete,

set in their ways, narrow-minded, overly realistic, and closed.

Clearly some jobs, such as marketing, copywriters, and creative workers,

are best filled with open-minded, imaginative people, while other jobs

require people to work on tasks in a conventional, tried and true fashion.

One would expect one’s accountant to regularly use commonly accepted

accounting standards but for the chef to come up with some new dishes, at

least some of the time. Again, matching the candidate’s level of openness

to experience with the requirements of the job is necessary for success on

the job.

Agreeableness is the degree to which an individual is likable, cooperative,

good-natured, forgiving, ready to accommodate to others, caring, trusting,

and tolerant. Disagreeable individuals are seen as thorny, prickly, intolerant,

unpleasant, tough, abrasive, critical, and cynical. Agreeable people see the

interpersonal glass as half full, with disagreeable ones see the glass as half

empty, or less. Agreeable people agree with the old maxims that say, “You

have to go along to get along.” and “While it’s nice to be important, it’s even

more important to be nice.”

Some jobs, like customer service representatives and receptionists require

a higher degree of agreeableness than do internal auditors and criminal

investigators, but even here a more careful analysis of the job requirements

within a particular job will uncover differences in different settings. The

requirements to be a successful customer service representative at

Nordstrom, the up-scale retailer, will be different from those at WalMart.

Conscientiousness is the degree to which a person is organized, planful,

meticulous, responsible, careful, hard-working, persevering, achievement-

oriented, and thorough. Highly conscientious persons have a well-

developed sense of ethics and integrity; they are able to develop realistic

action plans that are sensitive to time constraints and resource availability.

At its extreme levels conscientious persons can be experienced as strong-

willed, zealous people who are driven, puritanical, and moralistic, especially

about work. At very low levels of conscientiousness, there are carefree and

casual individuals who tend to live in the here and now. They are the free

spirits of society.

Different jobs require different levels of conscientiousness, although

probably all jobs require at least a modest level, i.e. expecting the person to

show up for work regularly, and on time. The level of conscientiousness

needed to be a successful brain surgeon is rather different than that re-

quired from a pre-school teacher or a creative artist. Has anyone ever seen

a neat artist’s studio? Not surprisingly, research tends to demonstrate that

conscientiousness is significantly related to successful job performance,

almost regardless of the nature of the job.

Applying the Big Five to the BestWork DATA Instrument

Five, although with a much sharper focus on job-related behaviors. First,

because of the singular importance of extroversion to on-the-job success,

assertiveness. And each of the other factors was renamed, using more

business-oriented labels. The resulting scales of the BestWork DATA
