Each Board Must Define their

Role in a Meaningful Manner

In theory, a board is accountable for

shaping strategy and approving the stra-

tegic plan; management is accountable

for implementing the plan.

In practice, the roles are intertwined.

Sometimes problematic implications

emerge. Words and phrases applied

to the manner boards and manage-

ment are expected to formulate risk-

mitigated strategy — i.e., partner, engage,

involve, contribute, shape, — easily confuse

the role expectations between manage-

ment and a board.

Yet, these words do seem to reflect

the current reality of the board-

management interdependence. Manage-

ment often wishes to control the stra-

tegic planning process, and present a

“bullet proof ” plan to the board hoping

for the board’s rapid grasp and approval

of the plan. In contrast, contemporary

directors grasp for greater participation

in the strategy planning process with in-

creased hands-on control and emotion -

al ownership of strategy. Directors may

not be content to be just advisors and

overseers; they may want to contribute

more actively and directly. Misplayed

roles lead to confusion and conflict.

The bottom line is that boards must

define their role in strategy management

in a meaningful manner. Boards should

expect well-informed directors, a healthy

board culture, and a tailored strategic plan-

ning policy which demarcates the role and

responsibilities of the board, those shared

roles, and finally management’s role and

responsibilities. A disciplined approach to

the process, often managed by the Chair,

fosters this appropriate contribution by

management and directors.

Competency Predictors: The Will and Skill Factors

Different capabilities are required of directors for thinking,

planning, and implementation—capabilities are separated

into will or skill factors.

“Will” is a function of commitment to serve versus a par-

ticular skill or competency to serve. We deal with commitment

separately from competency.

The Will to be Strategic.

It seems simple---exceptional directors must show up and be

prepared to contribute. Strategy management requires two

commitments: availability; preparedness.

Availability . Directors must be available for service—

inside and outside the board room. Directors must be regular-

ly available outside traditional board meetings for ad hoc con-

ference calls, telephonic meetings, and for independent study

and investigation. According the 2010-2011 NACD Director

Compensation Report, board meeting fees now represent only

3% of the total direct compensation for the Top 200 company

boards. Directors are paid for service, not meeting attendance.

Preparedness. Directors must show up well-prepped.

Strategy work requires independent investigation and synthe -

sis of a company’s core competencies, its value proposition vs.

competitors, the aging of its strategies, and emerging threats

and opportunities.

The Skill to be Strategic.

Strategy boils down to making decisions about where you can

win. Those teams equipped with better skill and process will

more often make better decisions.

Director Competency

Curiosity Desire to investigate and learn about familiar and novel matters which impact the company.

Directors who process information quickly typically have more “processing time” to explore other areas. They acquire new information easily and as- similate it into their world. The scope of their curiosity is determined by their taste for change. Innovative thinkers’ curiosity takes them far and wide. It is often in these foreign territories that breakthrough ideas are found. Directors who foster tradition and stability rather than change have a much more focused curiosity, directed at areas of immediate concern or that are directly connected to the core business.

Directors should be selected so that the board has a balance of curiosity consistent with its strategic aims.

Reasoning Systematic gathering, synthesizing, and evaluating information to decide what to do or not to do.

When gathering, synthesizing and evaluating information, directors with a high attention to detail can sometimes become lost in the details, missing the larger message. Conversely, directors who focus on the big picture can miss critical details. Effective boards recognize the value of each type of reason- ing and blend them together to get a clear and complete picture. Faster information processors have the added advantage of seeing the more complex relationships that may exist in the data. An interesting factor is the emotional sensitivity of the directors. Those with a lower level of sensitivity tend to approach such analysis with a positive expectation. When issues are discovered, they generally proceed to work through them with little emotional re- action. Directors with a high level of emotional sensitivity tend to look for what is wrong, anticipating problems.

A successful board must balance these talents in order to optimize their efforts.

Future Focus Those with the security of the present, whose interests, relationships, and energy are focused on long term priorities and achievement.

Speed of reasoning or processing determines a director’s field of focus. A camera’s zoom lens offers an excellent clarity at distance, but closer images are blurry. A macro lens brings a close-up of a flower into sharp resolution, but as the flower is moved away, it too becomes blurry. A normal lens is the best choice when the subject is not too far away or not too close. Similarly, fast processors focus on the future, having their best picture when talking about where the company is going to be. Moderate processors may discuss the future, but their clearest vision is of the tactical situation and what must be done in the near term. More deliberate processors focus on the present - what must be done now.

Directors are truly best at one field of focus. It is essential to understand the composition of the board to see what can best compliment their current vision.

Debate Skill Skillful use of factual and emotional arguments, and timing with the intent to persuade the board toward a preferred conclusion or point of view.

Assertiveness is a critical factor for any director. Highly assertive people use debate to understand and refine their ideas. They engage in persuasive ar- guments with other directors, and this practice serves to polish and clarify the thinking of all members of the board. Less assertive directors rarely par- ticipate in the debate, which presents two problems: First, their silence may be assumed to be acquiescence. Second, their opinions, however valuable, are not voiced.

Innovation Inclination, cleverness, and imagination to create and introduce new things.

Arguably, most directors subscribe to the value of innovation. Openness to new ideas is critical in today’s fast-changing world. Directors who welcome change, drive change and embrace new ideas are invaluable. It is vital to energize and sustain the “edge” of a board. It is important to see the scope of a Director’s comfort with innovation. Boards must walk a path between the foundation of success that built the enterprise and the opportunities of the future. They must filter out the most viable and meaningful new concepts and it is far easier to do this when there is a strong flow of ideas.

Again, the board must aim for balance when selecting Directors, ensuring a vital source of innovative thinking while maintaining a solid footing on the successes of the past.

Director Competency

Candor Being frank, open and sincere in speech or expression.

Highly assertive directors tend to be frank and direct. Directors who are less assertive wish to avoid challenges to their opinions. Therefore they tend to refrain from expressing them. When forced to speak out, they do so in the most general terms, disguising anything that may trigger a challenge.

Decisiveness Strength of confidence and commitment made to a course of action

Highly assertive directors are generally decisive. They accept the responsibility and are willing to deal with opposition to their decisions. Directors who are less assertive prefer getting a consensus before making important decisions. This can cripple a board’s effectiveness, making it difficult to take advan- tage of sudden opportunities. or to handle unexpected problems.

Stewardship Ethic that embodies responsible planning, monitoring, and management of assets, resources, and reputation.

Stewardship implies a responsibility that depends upon competency and personal values. One behavioral trait however, can play a major part. It affects time management and planning. A “plan-ful” Director brings that same nature to the task of preparing for the board meeting. They plan ahead. Their ef- ficient time management enables them to collect the necessary information and review it before the meeting. Their attention to details drives the thor- oughness of their preparation. Conversely, loose time management interferes with good intentions. That Director does not have the time to “get ready.” Their particular strength, rather than planning, is in reacting to whatever comes up. While that may be sufficient at times, it is definitely not the steward- ship that is expected.

Prudence Exercise of respect and compliance with rules and expected behavior.

Prudence is adherence to rules and standards. Rule-bound directors are strong in compliance matters and less comfortable in the vast “gray” areas with which boards deal. Rule-lax boards engender (too) risky decisions and often a cavalier disregard for statutes, policies, and code believing this can be nuanced, circumvented or ignored.

Senior leadership and boards are places where change agents are found and desired—and, change agents do change and circumvent rules. A Chairman is wise to understand the strength or absence of prudence within the board as this may become a pivotal matter for director selection and board process.

Problem solving Recognizing a problem or deficiency and recommending or directing corrective action.

Fast information processing is the basis for strong problem solving, particularly with complex or unfamiliar problems. Moderate processors use their ex- perience with proven solutions as their starting point.

The best boards are populated with moderate to highly decisive directors and led by and Chairman who drives fast forward but remains governed by judgment and sound decision process.

Coach & Counsel Application of experience and wisdom with the interpersonal sensitivity to engage other directors and management in constructive, remedial or devel- opmental conversations.

The ability of a Director to coach and counsel others depends primarily on their match with the person to be coached and counseled on various traits and abilities. The primary elements are general reasoning, assertiveness and openness to change and new ideas.

Analytic Speed Speed and insight for breaking down arguments, assumptions or analytic conclusions into basic principles or elements using logic and technical tools.

The most important factor in terms of analytic speed is the cognitive processing speed of the director. All things being equal, fast processors reach con- clusions more quickly; analyze presentations more quickly; and discern the key points in an argument more quickly. In a collaborative and open discussion, faster processors tend to move toward decisions more directly and more rapidly. Without understanding the reason for this, their alacrity may be inter- preted as impulsiveness instead of the advantage that it is.

Assertiveness Inclined toward bold, confident, and persistent assertion of ideas, conclusions, and points of view.

High levels of assertiveness enable Directors to speak out, challenging issues with which they do not agree. The challenge of defending or pushing forward their ideas enables them to test and refine them. Brilliant and experienced directors with a lower level of assertiveness frequently avoid potential con- frontations, holding their thoughts and ideas until a “better time.” Unfortunately, when many of the other directors are more assertive, that “better time” may not come.

Planning Skill in setting goals, committing scarce resources, developing detailed operational tactics, and coordinated work schedules to achieve goals.

The degree of planning that seems right to a Director depends upon his/her hard-wired personality. Some fear a plan can get bogged down with too many details, and other fear the lack of sufficient details.

Knowing the level of comfort with details and planning of each Director allows boards to allocate planning responsibilities to the greatest effect.

T olerance for Ambiguity Temperament for recognizing and accepting incomplete information while remaining actively goal focused.

For some directors, there is a definite “right way” of doing things and a “right way” of thinking. This tends to anchor their understanding, and consequent- ly it restricts their ability to accept ideas or processes that differ significantly from their view.

Boards rarely deal with easy problems. Most face significant degrees of ambiguity over these significantly challenging decisions.

Information Needs Need for detail, completeness, and thoroughness of analysis.

Directors who are hard-wired to need a higher level of details can struggle when information is incomplete. In contrast, others may discount the impor- tance of details, focusing on the big picture. They run the risk of missing key elements that may affect the decisions involved. Recognizing these facts, a board can see how to prepare directors for important discussions, meeting the quantity and quality informational needs of each person.

Engineer your board to be a competitive advantage

An effective board is comprised of accomplished executives,

each of whom bring their special knowledge and experience

to the work of that board. Each Director has typically been a

leader and decision maker in their own business. Now they

must work collaboratively, pooling diverse opinions and often

conflicting viewpoints, to craft a united position. It is critical

for each Director to respect the value that each member of the

board brings to the overall team.

The foundation of any board is the aggregate of the hard-

wired traits and cognitive abilities of each of the Directors.

This topology describes the extent of diversity among the

members in how they communicate; how they learn; what

is important to them in discussion; their focus of attention

in the business; their involvement in debates; their attitude

toward change; and much more. This topology can easily be

mapped with new assessment technology. This is called team-

engineering. With that kind of data, the board can design rules

of engagement that satisfy the needs of each director.

Team engineering also serves as an inventory of the

strengths and abilities of the board. As the needs of the en-

terprise change or whenever the board needs to add a direc-

tor, the team data can be used to see what strengths will be of

most benefit. This makes it possible to engineer the board’s ca-

pabilities to the specific needs of the business.
