A Practical & Actionable View of Culture

As companies of all sizes deal with the challenges of a changing workforce further complicated by the changing nature of the workplace, consultants have responded with the engaging, yet elusive concept of culture. This concept has been shown to have a remarkable elasticity, adapting to any number of business situations. My university had an excellent infirmary with an equally excellent medical team. They did have one curious thing however, which was revealed during a late night beer- fueled discussion of the treatments the students had experienced for various problems. Every student had at some time, received a prescription of light blue pills, The same light blue pills had served to treat stomach aches, headaches, sprained muscles, dizziness, fevers and spider bites. It was effectively a universal remedy. At least that is what it seemed to be. Too often in today’s business world, culture has become the light blue pill for consultants and business leaders trying to solve real problems. The travesty is that culture is a major factor contributing to the success or failure of an enterprise. It is important that it be defined more accurately with better tools and a model that can be more carefully crafted to the strategies of the business.

Companies actually have two distinct cultures. There is a values culture expressing the core beliefs of how the business essentially does business. Some examples : •Work-life balance •Long term customer relations •Warm and friendly workplace •Do whatever it takes to get the deal.

•The job comes first.

•Long hours are expected.

These are the values that are promoted and rewarded within the company. When

these values are inconsistent with those of the employees, productivity will always be negatively affected. Value cultures are usually set by the leadership of the company, and they are the ones that must change it if it is to be changed. Without a doubt, the values culture of a company must be clear and meaningful. It must resonate throughout everyone. But, knowing that, what actions can be done?

What we really want to do is understand how culture impacts the behaviors within

the enterprise. In fact, in addition to a values culture, it is clear that companies also have behavioral cultures. Culture has been described as the "personality of the company". BestWork is a sophisticated yet simple tool that compiles behavioral DATA, essentially the hard-wired personality traits and cognitive abilities of the people that make up the behavioral foundation of the company. This is the behavioral culture. When this DATA is aggregated showing the distribution within the organization or within parts of the organization, it is possible to see a map of the behavioral topology for that group. For the first time, the topology of the culture is quantifiably evident. Different groups with different cultures can be seen within the same company.

Example A : A software company with 410 employees was experiencing conflicts

and frustration among the various departments. Discussions attempting to resolve

these disputes were fruitless. The leadership explained that the culture of the

company was supposed to be one of friendly collaboration and cooperation. The

scope of the problems were originally highlighted in a company-wide survey of

work satisfaction. The results of the survey castigated management as not caring

and being deaf to the opinions of employees. A special consultant was called in,

and his first suggestion was to inventory the strengths of the workforce. This was done using the BestWork experience. The entire population of 410 employees was included. The importance of this example is the illustration of how critical it is to understand the behavioral cultures within any enterprise. No matter how much the concept of culture is discussed; no matter how it is promoted; no matter how many buttons are passed out and fine sounding phrases posted in the office, it cannot be practically realized without a behavioral culture to support it.
