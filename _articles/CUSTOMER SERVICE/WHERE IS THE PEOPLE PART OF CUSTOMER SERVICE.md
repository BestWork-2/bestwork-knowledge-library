Where Is the People Part of Customer Service?

Yesterday, I visited one of those electronics superstores with my son. We

were on a combined mission to buy a CD player for my wife’s piano studio,

compare prices on flat screen tv’s, and generally scout out new ways for

him to amaze me with his superior knowledge of today’s technology. This

store was ideal for that with seemingly acres of products of all types. Not

only was there a vast selection, but their prices were as good or better than

online. We spent almost two hours deciding on a CD player and

discovering things we never knew we needed. During our time there, after

the perfunctory greeting at the door, only one associate spoke to us until

we checked out. There were associates in every department, but none

greeted us, much less engage us to see if they could assist us. We even

said, “hello”, to one as we passed him in an aisle, but he did not respond. It

was sort of a retail version of the Borg Cube from Star Trek, where the Borg

did not recognize the presence of intruders. When we did carry our self-

selected items to the checkout area, we found a remarkably impersonal

experience, punctuated by an equally impersonal “Thank you for shopping

at ….” In filling out the customer survey to the store manager, I observed

that when I have a particular need, their merchandise selection and pricing

is sufficient for me to consider them. However, only PEOPLE connecting

with me can command my loyalty. It is that extra bit of emotional content

and personal appreciation that is a powerful differentiation in a world of

online price shopping and one-click for delivery to my door. This is not

found in mission statements on laminated cards or in set phrases parroted

to customers. It is found in associates whose hard-wired personality traits

cause them to project warmth, friendliness and enthusiasm to everyone

they meet, whether they are customers, neighbors or strangers in the

grocery store. In a world with BestWork, it is easy to select these PEOPLE.
