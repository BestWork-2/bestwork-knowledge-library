Thoughts on Unconscious Bias in Hiring

When a company is hiring a new employee, their principal

objective is to obtain a particular set of job behaviors. In baseball,

a team is not looking for another baseball player. They are

seeking a 90 mph fastball, 30+ home runs, the ability to turn a

double play or a similar specific ability. Similarly, the company is

seeking the ability to close a sale, compliance with safety

procedures, friendly interaction with customers or other specific

job behaviors. The hiring manager or recruiter is trying their best

to get the right person for the job. They do not want to make a

mistake, but the information they have to work with in that

endeavor is limited at best, and often, quite misleading. They look

for whatever clues that are visible, regardless of the real

relevance to the decision. (e.g. MBA’s, 10 years of experience, a

particular skill, appearance, communication, etc.) Some of these

are checkoffs, such as a degree or years of experience. Others

are subjective, being consciously or unconsciously evaluated by

the interviewer.

Now it is important to step away from the interview room for a

moment and consider the things that make human beings feel

comfortable and secure. Is it with people who are similar to them

in appearance, in language and speech, in dress, in levels of

education, in mannerisms and more? When those things are

different, is the feeling more or less comfortable? This is easily

seen when two people meet on a plane or at a social event.

Immediately they begin to seek commonalities. That conscious

and unconscious level of comfort with the job candidate is a

powerful factor in any decisions. Lacking an unbiased reference

But what about diversity, and all the discussions and workshops

on its importance? The glitch is that diversity remains a concept,

one that can be applauded and encouraged, but that lacks the

immediacy and intimacy of that search for commonality. When

companies attempt to impose diversity with percentages of hires,

the irony is that such programs themselves become tools for bias.

Whenever a factor takes precedence over the fit with the

necessary job behaviors, it must inevitably have a negative

impact on the enterprise.

Now, there is a solution that can dramatically reduce bias,

conscious and unconscious, in hiring decisions. Every human

being has hard-wired traits and abilities that are directly related to

job behaviors. The latest generations of psychometric instruments

can measure those traits and abilities accurately and easily. Until

now, accessing that information was dependent upon professional

interpretation or generalized reports comparing job candidates to

templates created by testing “top performers” in some job.

Unfortunately, the construction of these templates was often

prone to bias in the selection of the “top performers.”

The newest applications for this data describe the job candidate’s

ability to deliver any specific job behavior. The hiring manager or

recruiter can quickly see if the candidate CAN or CANNOT

perform that job behavior at the level needed for that specific job.

This can even be done prior to meeting the candidate or reading

their resumé. Bad hires can be screened out immediately, leaving

only candidates that CAN do the job to some level remaining.

There are countless implications to what this disruptive innovation

means to talent acquisition. The purpose of this paper is to

explore what it means in terms of conscious or unconscious bias

in hiring practices. This introduces a binary decision into the

screening and selection process. It is readily apparent whether or

not the candidate can deliver the critical job behaviors for a

specific job. This is primary information for the decision. It is

objective, without any bias. It does not remove conscious or

deliberate bias, but it does tend to supersede any unconscious

bias. Data has made the decision less risky. “Bad hires” become

rare occurrences, and this experience of focusing on job

capabilities, over time, will even begin to blur conscious bias. It is

not a magic wand or a quick fix, but more importantly, it changes

the foundational elements of the decision process. To paraphrase

Papa John, “Better data…better decisions.” The data that is

added displaces bias and that is the first step toward positive

change. If companies use job behavior data to place the right

person in the right job, many diversity issues go away, and even

those that remain have simpler solutions.
