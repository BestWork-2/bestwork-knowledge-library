Knowing the Right Roles in an Entrepreneurial World

Prepared for the International Forum on Entrepreneurship, 2017, Katmandhu, Nepal

Entrepreneurial businesses are a critical driving force behind the economies of every country throughout the world. Their contribution to employment and the GDP is vital, but its true potential has historically been limited by the failure rate of these enterprises. It is not enough to start an entrepreneurial business. It must be sustainable. It must be able to continue. After twenty years of working with entrepreneurial businesses and correlating their performance with DATA on the hard-wired personality traits and cognitive abilities of their founders, executives and key employees, it is clear that most of these failures are unnecessary and easily preventable. The objective was to develop a tool to enable any entrepreneur to have a specific inventory of his team’s job behavior capabilities. With that information, the entrepreneur can avoid putting people into roles they cannot perform.

Every entrepreneurial business depends upon a limited number of people to play key roles.

Often, one person must attempt to fill several roles. Rarely is this successful, as each role requires strengths and abilities that may be quite different from those required by another role. In studying hundreds of entrepreneurial companies that were struggling despite having a sound idea and a ready market, DATA inventories revealed salespeople who could not sell; managers who could not manage; executive teams that could not innovate; and production managers who could not recognize quality problems. The amazing thing was that when the DATA revealed the cause of the problem, the solutions were relatively straightforward and simple to execute. Just as physicians today use MRI’s to see previously hidden causes of medical problems, entrepreneurs can use this new DATA to understand the cause of their problems. The goal now is to make this tool available for all entrepreneurs and to make it available for more extensive research by other parties.

Example: Unsuccessful Sales Team

A medium-sized company had developed and patented several significant innovations in their industry. These innovations had the potential to give the company a dominant position in their market. Despite this promise, for the first few years, the company struggled to produce sales.

Why were they failing?

First, the company’s product used a highly sophisticated technology to deliver customized solutions. Salespeople had to master this complicated product knowledge and be able to explain it to potential customers. In addition, the sales strategy was to offer an integrated solution that touched many areas within each customer. This meant that multiple individuals within the prospect must each be persuaded to make a buying decision. The company felt that this approach gave them a competitive advantage over their competition. What was not apparent at that time was the extent to which this strategy challenged the job capabilities of the sales team.

This was a complex sale, and learning the necessary product knowledge demanded the ability to learn and process information very quickly. Persuading several different buyers required a high level of assertiveness and the willingness to deal with confrontation and objections of different kinds. DATA was used to inventory the relative strengths and abilities of the sales team. Four of these salespeople are not persuasive (red squares), and therefore cannot be successful at closing sales.

The DATA strengths inventory clearly shows that only two of the ten salespeople have any chance of being successful. It is important to note that the eight unsuccessful ones were educated, hard working, and likable employees. They received considerable training, including specialized sales training. Yet the inability of the company to recognize that only 20% of their sales team could sell their products almost led to their complete failure. Example #2 Small Training & Consulting Company

After a successful career in a large chemical company, one of the senior executives started his own business doing what he loved best, training and consulting. He bought a license for an outstanding program of training materials and went through the appropriate certifications with top marks. A few of his close friends engaged his services, and his work in helping them was exceptional. Unfortunately, as the next two years went by, new engagements were not realized, despite the fact that he regularly attended networking events and followed up with many leads.

DATA was used to map his hard-wired strengths and abilities so that they could be compared to those that were critical to the training and consulting business. In terms of operating the business and delivering excellent training and consulting services, his strengths were perfect. However, training and consulting engagements must be sold. Entrepreneurs can usually fill one or sometimes a few other roles in their business. No one can fill all of the roles needed to operate the business. It is vital to know which roles are in jeopardy or which ones need help. DATA provides that knowledge, and it can ensure that others members of the entrepreneurial team fit their roles.

A Few More Thoughts

There have been many opinions and theories put forth on the performance of entrepreneurial businesses. Two of the more prominent ones revolve around the concepts of innovation and entrepreneurial culture. While these have sparked rich discussions and a positive enthusiasm for action, it is difficult to identify tangible results in productivity or profitability. The concepts are powerful and no one really challenges their importance to entrepreneurs but the practical application has been elusive. Using DATA to analyze these concepts has produced some remarkable insights that do lead to practical application within entrepreneurial businesses.

Bringing the concept of innovation into practical reality requires an understanding of its component parts. The most basic of these is a hard-wired personality trait that describes how open an individual is to new experiences or new ideas. The population is distributed on this scale along a normal bell curve. At one extreme, innovation is wide open without regard to conventional thinking or traditional practices. This is the source of disruptive ideas that challenge what exists and the way things have always been done. New technologies and radically new products spring from this kind of thinking. At the other extreme of the innovation bell curve are deeply rooted conventions that are well established. At that end of the scale, innovation means a slightly different color or simple additional feature to an old product. All of the individuals along this scale are innovative, but the scale of their innovation is quite different.

Entrepreneurial businesses must incorporate talent from both ends of the scale if they are to be sustainable. It is not enough to have the great breakthrough idea. The idea must be monetized with systems and procedures that enable its consistent and profitable delivery. That is the job of the more conservative end of the bell curve. Without this kind of support, innovative entrepreneurs can become a fountain of ideas that never reach maturity.

Another concept that is promoted as being important is that of fostering an entrepreneurial culture. There is no doubt that the concept sounds appealing, and that it seems to make sense.

The concept lends itself to inspirational speeches and discussions. The catch comes when it comes time to take action. No one is debating the value of an entrepreneurial culture. Everyone is for it. Perhaps the disconnect is that culture is a better way of thinking or an attitude. What entrepreneurs need is a better way of doing.

These case studies and examples of actual entrepreneurial engagements illustrate the practical and pragmatic application of DATA in diagnosing failure points in real businesses, and in identifying correctional protocols to resolve the problems. There are countless subgroups of entrepreneurial businesses: rural/urban; male/female; high growth economies/low growth economies and more. While the economic environments and the availability of resources may vary, each business must be able to deliver a similar set of job behaviors in order to prosper.

These include: selling the goods or the services offered by the business; managing the operation of the business; providing customer service; organizing and planning; handling financial matters; recruiting talent; marketing and more. DATA provides a common language for these job behaviors. Regardless of the circumstances, the DATA can reveal the strengths and weaknesses of the business to deliver them. With that knowledge, actions can be taken that directly impact the success probabilities of the enterprise.

Of course, there are many factors that affect entrepreneurial businesses: government regulations, the economic environment, access to capital and others. These, however, are not in the control of the entrepreneur. DATA is in their control, and because of that, it offers a ready tool that can be applied without the need for outside help. It enables the entrepreneur to ensure that the operational strengths of the team can support the operational strategies of the enterprise.
