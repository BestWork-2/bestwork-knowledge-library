A Better Understanding of Introverts & Extroverts

The problem with generalized terminology is that while it is not exactly

wrong, it is not exactly right. An excellent example of this is the categorizing

of people into two groups : introverts & extroverts. Using these terms is

fairly harmless, as sorting out potential guests for the party…happier

parties have more extroverts. The introverts will probably be happier at

home anyway. That is, unless their spouse is an extrovert who should really

be invited. Then of course, there are the ambiverts. You cannot go wrong

with them in any event.

Unfortunately, similarly simplistic sorting takes place in the business world.

Too often, the extroverts become known as “people oriented”, and the

introvert is classified as “not really a people person”. What does it really

say? How does it affect job performance.

BestWork measures the hard-wired personality trait that is the basis for

people-related behaviors. It then translates that into specific job behaviors

found in various positions. Normative measures show where the individual

is on a normal bell curve. This enables the comparison of the behaviors of

different individuals. Half of the population is found in the middle of the bell

curve. These are the ambiverts, with a balance of each type of behavior

while lacking any extremes. Imagine the set of basketball players who are

neither exceptionally tall or exceptionally short. With personality traits, the

ambiverts can usually perform adequately, although not exceptionally.

The question is what behaviors are necessary or valuable in various jobs.

Are the extremes necessary or desirable for certain jobs?

•Sales which depend upon enthusiastic and high energy

presentations need an extreme extrovert.

•Sales which depend upon networking and prospecting need

extreme extroverts.

•Sales territories that require spending a lot of time alone or

traveling are best for introverts.

•Workspaces where interaction with coworkers is continual and

intense are best with extroverts.

•Solitary workspaces with little interaction with customers or

coworkers are best with introverts.

•Extrovert managers like meetings.

•Introvert managers have few meetings.

•Introvert supervisors have limited time on the factory floor,

working in their office most of the time.

•Extrovert supervisors are constantly walking around engaging

their team.

•Extrovert waitstaff in a restaurant create a warm and friendly

feeling with guests, important to the 2 and 4 top tables.

•Introvert waitstaff in a restaurant seem cool and impersonal.

They are best with the large table groups.

•Introvert retail staff offer little in the way of a pleasant shopping

experience.

These are just some examples to demonstrate the direct relationship

between job behaviors and one personality trait. Talent management also

depends upon that information.

•Extreme extroverts do not listen well. While someone else is

talking, the extrovert is planning what they are going to say when

they talk. Taking notes is an effective way to ensure the level of

listening.

•Extroverts tell stories. In fact, whatever another person say,

probably triggers a “me too” story for the extrovert.

•Extroverts also use their stories to work out their understanding

of something.

•Introverts listen well but they often do not engage in the

discussion, waiting for a quieter time.

•Extroverts have many friends of many kinds. Introverts have few

friends but those tend to be close.
