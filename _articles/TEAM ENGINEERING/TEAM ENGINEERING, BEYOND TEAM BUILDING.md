Team Engineering

Beyond Team Building

It is time to go beyond simple team building. Virtually every executive in

the United States has experienced a DISC or Myers-Briggs-based team

building workshop at some point in their career. They are generally fun,

and it is always fascinating to see what everyone else is. They rarely have

any long lasting impact on team performance however. Moreover, the DISC

or MBTI event really does not connect with any other initiatives. The good

news is that in the over half a century since these assessments were origi-

nally developed, the science of psychometrics has made quantum leaps in

terms of capabilities. Today, better DATA, including both personality traits

and cognitive abilities, enables the engineering of highly effective teams.

The complete topology of teams can be mapped out so that anyone can see

the advantages and issues for any team. That same DATA is used for selec-

tion, training, succession planning, performance management and much

more. No longer is a team program merely an event.

Using the BestWork Strengths Inventory

The BestWork Team Strengths Inventory is the cornerstone of the Team En-

gineering process. It displays the scores of individuals in a table format

representing the normal distribution of the scores compared to the general

working population. For example, the box at each end of the table indi -

cates the 10% of people who score in that range on that particular scale. The

second and fifth boxes represent 15% of the population. The two middle

boxes each represent 25% of people typically scoring in that range.

In plain terms, the BestWork Team Strengths Inventory is a simple means

of comparing the team characteristics of an individual to those of other

team members. It provides an objective measurement of common team-re-

lated behaviors. Rather than simply describing a team characteristic as “de-

tail-oriented” or “controlling”, the BestWork Team Strengths Inventory al-

lows the user to see exactly the degree of “details” or the degree of “con-

trol” relative to the rest of the world. More importantly, the DATA allows

the user to see these characteristics with regard to the team as a whole.

With experience, many BestWork team consultants favor the DATA because

a quick look gives them a great deal of information. However, unlike other

team building tools, BestWork does not require learning any special models

or terminology. The mission of BestWork is to make the behavioral infor-

mation readily available and actionable for anyone. BestWork offers a series

of tools that address the common challenges of teams, such as communica-

tion, collaboration and critical thinking. The purpose of this paper is to in-

struct clients and consultants in how to apply the DATA in a few of the

most valuable areas.

MACRO ANALYSIS OF WORK GROUPS

Every business has a Talent Warehouse, which is their human resources.

The talents in that warehouse represent the human capital of the enterprise.

The ability of the business to effectively use the potential of that human

capital is directly related to how well they understand the inventory of job

competencies in each work group. Skills and experience have proven to be

dangerously inadequate for this. The fact that a person played baseball in

the majors for ten years is dramatically different in value to a record show-

ing an ERA of 1.8 and a won-loss record of 18-4 for the Red Sox. As infor-

mation becomes more specific, it enables more effective business decisions.

BestWork produces exactly that level of information on the job capabilities

of each individual. When that information is collected and compiled into a

composite of the job capabilities of the work group, it is possible to get a

clear picture of what the team’s actual capabilities are and more important-

ly, where there are weakness or potential issues to executing the current

strategies.

The following three examples will demonstrate the power of these team

engineering composites to provide insights into various types of work

groups.

EXECUTIVE TEAM ANALYSIS

This company pioneered a space within the manufacturing industry, quick-

ly growing to 100 million in revenue. Seven years later, they found them-

selves #3 in the industry and on their way to #4, despite strong margins,

excellent customer reviews, high quality, and on time performance. The

CEO, President, CFO, and VP of Sales completed the BestWork Experience,

and the results were compiled into the following composite chart. The

number represents how many of the four executives fall into that box.

These five scales each display one of the five clues to the cause of the com-

pany’s decline in market share.

Immediate vs Strategic

First, all four executives tended to focus their attention and energies on tac-

tical issues, those impacting the current quarter and perhaps the following

one. In fact, the company was excelling in tactical performance: profit mar-

gins, customer service, and quality products. Executives who are more

strategic tend to focus their attention and energies on issues impacting the

Following vs Controlling

All four of the top executive were highly controlling, quick to state their

opinions and to confront any challenges to that opinion. There is a lot of

strength in this type of management team, but defending opinions is not

the same things as discussing and sharing ideas. It requires clear knowl -

edge of how each person is wired, supported by a communication structure

that channels the confrontations into collaboration for this kind of team to

really prosper without squandering their energy with internal bickering.

Individual Effort vs Collective Effort

The fourth trait scale compounded this problem. Each of the executives

was individually-oriented. In other words, they wanted to excel, to win, to

be the best, and to be right. They did not want to fail, to lose, to not be the

best, or to be wrong. Their view of achieving these things was based on

their own individual effort rather than the collective efforts of the team.

The company was faced with losing market share and each one wanted to

know why and each one wanted to avoid the blame. With their strengths of

assertiveness, they fought to be right and defended their actions. The ener-

gy of the company was turned away from the marketplace and spent on

these internal blame-storming sessions.

tend to follow rules. This is partly because people who are not detail-ori-

ented and who do not like convention and regulations do not normally ap-

ply for jobs in banking. The head teller had been an exceptional teller, and

that performance had resulted in her promotion to the supervisory role.

Unfortunately, in that role, her performance had been unsatisfactory. There

were tardiness issues among her team, and morale was poor, which was

sometimes reflected in their customer interactions.

The job of a supervisor is filled with confrontation. They must give feed-

back on the performance of the employees in order for the employees to

improve that performance. The feedback must be specific. “Try to do bet-

ter” or “This is not acceptable” offer no clues for the employee on either

what is wrong or what to do differently. When feedback is generalized, the

employee’s potential for growth is stunted. It is also frustrating for the

manager. They gave the poor feedback as their best effort to encourage or

motivate the employee. When nothing happens as a result, the anticipated

confrontation only grows larger. At last, when faced with upper manage-

ment’s demand for greater performance from their team, the supervisor

erupts unexpectedly, like a dormant volcano, perhaps citing a litany of per-

formance errors. Up until that time, the employee may have only heard

some generalized comments or even mild encouragement. Morale is the

victim of such delayed feedback, and turnover is a likely outcome.

Five members of the head teller’s team are higher in terms of being direct

and challenging tough things. On a subconscious level, this difference pre-

cluded any meaningful feedback to these five tellers. The resulting situa-

tion was exactly as described above. Supervisors or managers can effective-

ly supervise individuals who are equal to or lower than they are on these
