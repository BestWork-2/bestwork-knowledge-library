Interested…Then Students, Beware

Cars from the 1950’s have beautiful curves and fantastic chrome bumpers

that never fail to catch the eye, although the chance of seeing one outside

of an automobile show is rare. The 50’s were also the era of the hula hoop,

the poodle skirt and the Holland career interest model. Unfortunately, while

poodle skirts and hula hoops have moved into the novelty category, the

concept of interests as being the key to career success lives on. In fact, it

has morphed into a myriad of clever surveys promising to connect any

student’s interests with the job of their dreams, ensuring academic focus

and career satisfaction. The power of the Internet has given birth to

countless incarnations of the interest model, each with engaging graphics

and a slick user interface.

There is just one disastrous problem with this cavalcade of career

direction…it doesn’t work quite like they say. In fact, if interest surveys

fell under the FDA, there would be more disclaimers than you see in the

wonder drugs advertised on TV. Something such as this would be

appropriate: This information should not be used if the student is extremely

talented because misdirection can occur. If the student is not highly

knowledgeable about the world of possible jobs and the specific day-to-day

elements of those jobs, their answers could be misinterpreted. The fact that

a student may be interested in a particular vocation is in no way connected

with their ability to either succeed in that vocation or even to enjoy the

actual practice of that vocation. At no times is this information to be used to

make significant financial decisions such as choosing which college to

attend or to select a particular course of study. This information should not

be used without the supervision of a professional counselor or someone

who actually knows what they are doing instead of relying on this outdated,

although “interesting,” exercise.

Schools would be appalled if asked to use 10-year old textbooks. The

content, concepts and even the delivery methods are continually evolving.

The same is true of psychology’s understanding of how the traits, abilities

and interests of an individual are correlated with job performance and job

satisfaction. Since the 1950’s, it has been established that human beings

have hard-wired personality traits. These traits are the foundation of how

students perform and thrive in different majors yet struggle and are

frustrated in others. More importantly, these traits are the foundation of how

the students will perform the job behaviors that are critical for success in
