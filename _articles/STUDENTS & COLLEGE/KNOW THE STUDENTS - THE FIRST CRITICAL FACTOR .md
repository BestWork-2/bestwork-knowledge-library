Know the Students - The First Critical Factor

There are no shortages of sincere and passionate people discussing how

to transform education to meet the needs of today’s world. The businesses

are different. The pace of business is different. The people have different

options and different challenges. These are intelligent discussions, but they

lack accurate and current information on the key players…the students.

The problems are well-defined and there are countless statistics to quantify

the failures in the cost of the education and its poor contribution to the

employability of the workforce. Millions of students are misdirected into

majors that have little or no connection to a career path. The average

student in the USA changes majors three times. Each change adds

thousands of dollars to the cost which adds to student debts. College is

heralded as the true path, completely discounting hundreds of fulfilling

occupations that do not need college degrees. Trades cry out for more

apprentices. Advice and counseling is found in every school, in outside

services and online. Why then, are over 70% of employed adults

unsatisfied with their jobs. What if one of the basic assumptions is wrong?

The current educational methodology has its roots in the Industrial

Revolution. Educate the masses and train them in classes. The assumption

is that anyone can learn anything and therefore anyone can do anything if

they try hard enough. The variable to success was seen as motivation or

grit. In the 80’s, psychologists pursuing multiple avenues of research, all

concluded that human beings have hard-wired personality traits that are

foundational to how they think, learn and behave. This shattered the

existing paradigms of hiring and training. It meant that about half of the

people would never be able to sell despite whatever training they had. It

meant that about half the people would never be successful managers

despite having an MBA. To know that they had placed people into the right

Cognitive abilities and their effect on job performance had been established

in the 1940’s. While schools appeared to be aware of how cognitive

abilities could affect student performance in the classroom, the actual

impact was lost in a flood of diverse theories. Sadly, the concept of

intelligence was mistakenly seen as separating the smart kids from the

dumb kids. Grades and scholastic test scores were accepted as indicators

of intelligence. Schools tinkered with styles of learning. Math programs

were redesigned. Textbooks were rewritten. The focus of these efforts was

external to the students. Further clouding the issues was the long overdue

attention on equal access to education. This lead to the principle of treating

all students as being the same. The intentions were right but the unique

strengths of each student were lost. Career decisions became based on

starting salaries or suggestions from parents and friends. Schools used

archaic tools from the 40’s to help students find a place in a digital world.

An industry of college counselors was born, often charging thousands of

dollars and using the same outdated methodologies. The media touted

STEM careers as the promised land of digital careers, despite the fact that

more than half of the students lack the traits and abilities for such a career.

Led by the idea that any student could do anything if they worked hard

enough, no one wanted to point out such things. College debt reached a

trillion dollars. Average time to complete a four year degree rose to just

under six years. Half the doctors said they wish they had done something

else, as did half of the lawyers. Mentors tried to mentor students to

succeed in the wrong majors. The educational world had lost touch with its

customers.

The same logic follows in education. A striking example of this is found by

examining the effects of a single factor, which is general reasoning or fluid

intelligence.This cognitive ability is the determining element for speed of

learning or how quickly a person can acquire new information. This is

dramatically different from traditional thinking about IQ’s. The assumption

with IQ’s is that higher is better. That is not the case with speed of learning.

Many Ph.D.’s learn slowly, as do most research scientists who are brilliant

in their field. Effectiveness in many jobs depends upon the ability to learn

and process information slowly. Other jobs depend upon fast learning

speed. This speed of learning can be seen as a normal distribution in the

population.

Speed of learning is a fact. It is a primary variable in how a person learns

and what kinds of information they can learn. There are other factors that

play key roles in learning, but for the sake of simplicity, the focus is on this

single fact and how it impacts teaching effectiveness in a typical high

school or college classroom.

If the teacher/professor learns at the B level, most of the class is well-

matched to that. Some A’s will struggle at times, and some C’s will be bored

and distracted with the slower pace of the teacher’s delivery.

If the teacher/professor learns at the C level, the C’s are going to be

engaged. About half of the B’s will struggle at times, and the A’s will be lost

much of the time.

If the teacher/professor learns at the A level, the A’s are engaged and can

follow the teacher. About half of the B’s are engaged, and half are not

engaged all the time. The C’s are completely bored and disinterested, often

receiving poor grades despite their ability to learn quickly.

All students have the ability to learn, but not all learn at the same speed.

This is reality and when it is acknowledged, simple solutions become clear.

A sample of twenty high school teachers were shown the distribution of one

of their classes on the speed of learning scale. 3 students in the class learn

slowly. 9 students learn at an average speed. 3 students learn quickly.

The teachers were asked what they could do with that fact. Almost

immediately they said, “That’s easy. Ask the quick learners challenging

questions to engage their attention. Check on the slow learners and

provide additional resources for them.”

This is a simple example of a common problem in virtually every

classroom. The problem is not the teacher or the methods. It is not

understanding the unique strengths of each student and bundling all of

them into the same category. This is only one factor and its importance is

huge. There are other factors that are equally important. Without

understanding all of them, any plan to improve educational results are no

more than wishes. Any meaningful improvement must start with knowing

the strengths of each student.
