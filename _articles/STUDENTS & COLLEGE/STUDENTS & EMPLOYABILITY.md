Students & Employability

Employability has two parts. The most important is the student’s ability to

perform the job in question successfully. The second is the employer’s

ability to accurately recognize that ability. In the first case, far too much

emphasis has been on the knowledge and education received by the

student. In the second case, subjective opinions and interviews have been

the primary means of evaluating the student’s viability in the job. In an age

of technology and science, the question of employability can easily be

answered with behavioral and cognitive DATA. 1

A study of scholarship students at a major university revealed that one

third of the students had chosen majors that were not supported by their

hard-wired personality traits and cognitive abilities. In other words, they

were short people who had chosen basketball as their sport. They could

work hard and learn everything about the game. Still, they would have put

exceptional effort just to be adequate. They would never excel. A student

who was not accurate with details and who did not like to follow

procedures would face similar challenges in the financial world. Brilliant

students who breeze through their classes often are aiming at careers that

consist of relatively routine tasks with little of the challenges they need to

remain engaged. Highly creative students are steered into accounting or

civil engineering by well-meaning parents with the assurance that

accountants and civil engineers “always have work.” While that parental

statement may be true, it has little relevance for creative students whose

true strengths are ignored.

As measured with current psychometric science, these personality traits and cognitive abilities 1 do not change with training, coaching or incentives.

## Page 2

It is a telling statistic that over 70% of employed people wish they could

do something else besides the job they currently hold. The average

college student changes majors three times. Clearly there is a disconnect

between students and careers. Two things cause this. First, the majority of

students are playing a version of Pin the Tail on the Donkey. Essentially

blindfolded by a lack of knowledge about the actual realities of few, if any,

jobs, they blindly follow the shouts of their parents and the media touting

the latest “hot” careers. TV shows and movies add to the mix. With this

cacophony of information, it would take a miracle for a student to find a

match to their best career path.

The second wave of misdirection comes from the unbelievably outdated,

and ineffective assortment of assessments that career counselors,

educational institutions and well-meaning parents have been sold. They

would cringe at the thought of being stuck with an older cell phone but

are okay providing guidance to students with such antiques as DISC (70+

yrs. old), Myers-Briggs (50+ yrs. old) or Predictive Index (50+ yrs. old).

These and many others are based on old personality theories that have

long since been replaced with more advanced knowledge. The good news

is that hard-wired personality traits and cognitive abilities are easily

measured with newer applications and they offers easy to understand

information that everyone can use.

This DATA becomes the bridge connecting the student’s ability to perform

the job with the actual behaviors needed for that job. It is a common

language that is easily understood by both parties. Employability becomes

a specific and measurable factor that serves both students and employers.

That same measurability creates a metric that can be used to analyze

groups of students to understand the potential impact of various course

offerings. The current drive emphasizing STEM careers is challenged by

the fact that fewer than half of all students have the behavioral and

cognitive strengths needed for STEM careers. The other students have an

infinite number of other options that are supported by their strengths, and

only STEM-type jobs are a poor fit.

## Page 3

The reality is that both the basketball coach and the players recognize the

critical importance of height in that game. Easily understandable

information on behavioral and cognitive abilities and how they relate to

specific careers and jobs enables the same student-driven insights into

their own employability. With DATA, employers can recognize that same

employability and act with more confidence in their hiring decisions. Cities

can plan training programs that target the greatest potential in their

workforce.
