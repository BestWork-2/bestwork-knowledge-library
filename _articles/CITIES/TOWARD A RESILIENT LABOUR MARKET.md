Toward a Resilient Labour Market

From the OECD 8 July 2020

The COVID-19 pandemic has triggered one of the worst jobs crises

since the Great Depression. There is a real danger that the crisis will

increase poverty and widen inequalities, with the impact felt for years

to come. Countries now need to do everything they can to stop this

jobs crisis from turning into a social crisis. Reconstructing a better

and more resilient labour market is an essential investment in the

future and in future generations.

COVID-19 is real. The potential danger posed by its impact on jobs is real.

The need for a solution is also real. The promise of a better and more

resilient labour market is only a concept. It is an important and vital concept

but it must ignite action that will drive real results. Only action can make

that resiliency real. Defining people by their jobs creates a brittle labour

market. If flight attendants are furloughed or released, their ability to find

new flight attendant jobs is unlikely. Old job titles cannot connect job

seekers to new jobs. At a time when many industries are shrinking, a

resumé of excellent although outdated experience has little value in the job

market. A radically new vocabulary must be created to define workers by

their intrinsic strengths that do not change with the labour market and that

connect with any job. These are the cognitive abilities and hard-wired

personality traits that are stable in each person. Today these are easily

measurable, providing data that truly transforms the possibilities of

employability and job economies. The jobs may change, but these

characteristics remain essentially the same. Every individual has strengths

that can be applied to a myriad of jobs. Acquiring new knowledge may be

necessary, but when the right combination of critical personality traits and

cognitive abilities are available, that can easily be done. When the right

person is in the right job, experience can be acquired quickly.

An examination of the term resilience and its practical meaning in the

business world is important. It must be understood in two different, but

related ways: First, in terms of each individual member of the workforce,

and second, in terms of a City’s workforce as a whole. Any strategic

decision on labour is dramatically enhanced with the macro view of

resilience. Tactical plans and actions must be based on this information if

they are to have any real value or expectation of impact on the labour

crisis. The City’s resilience is, of course, based on the collective resilience

of the individual members of its workforce. Therefore, the beginning is

found in understanding the personal resilience of the individual worker.

One third of the population deals with change easily. Their circumstances

may be difficult, but their inherent flexibility and adaptability may be

described as resilience. One third of the population struggles with change.

They look for the old solutions to work again. They look for the same job

they have always had. They are not resilient.

Half of the population has a bias for action. Upon losing one job, they

immediately go out and persuade someone else to hire them. It may not be

the same job, but they are back working and their bias for action has them

continually alert for a better opportunity. Their ability to deal with

confrontation and difficulties and to act may be described as resilience. Half

of the population hesitates to act. They avoid any confrontation and they

cannot persuade others. They are far from being described as resilient.

If in fact, resilience is a characteristic enjoyed by only a fraction of the

population, how can a labour market be made resilient? How can resilient

Cities be realized? Ironically, the answer lies within the same hard-wired

strengths and abilities that determine the resilience of individuals. On a

macro level, that information unlocks extraordinary potentials for Cities.

First, it is useful to study a parallel in the world of sports. In the 1950’s,

managers of professional soccer teams and baseball teams made player

decisions with only the most basic data. Much of the decisions were based

on how the player was playing in the last game. Today, the performance

data on each player is extraordinary, revealing statistics on a myriad of

permutations with different competition, different scores, different weather

and other factors. For the managers, this information has given them an

agility in positioning their players in the most effective ways. As their

knowledge of each player’s capabilities became more specific, they saw

more clearly how to deploy them to the best advantage. That same

performance data proscribed the most beneficial training for each player.

Imagine a City’s workforce with each person’s unique strengths and

abilities known in easily understood behavioral terms. Imagine that jobs

within that City being defined and described using the same behavioral

terms and metrics. Jobs and job seekers could easily be matched. The

same data would guide the training needs for each person. If a particular

industry failed, the employees would not be shunted into a common

reskilling effort destined to fail with most of the people. Instead, the

behavioral metrics would be used to match them with new jobs and to

provide training opportunities matched to their capabilities. Having an

effective inventory of the strengths of the workforce, the City could

strategically plan the recruitment of industries that needed those strengths.

Their training and education dollars could be directed toward building for

the future rather than chasing problems.

It is a time of challenges, but it is also a time of opportunities. COVID-19

has given an impetus to transform the world of labour and business. The

tools are at hand to marry the language of job capabilities with the

behaviors needed in every job. It is a language that is understood by the

candidates, the recruiters, the trainers and the Cities. This commonality of

understanding enables the collective efforts and synergies that have long

been needed.
